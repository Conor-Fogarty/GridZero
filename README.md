CS 5150 Grid Zero WriteUp
By Conor Fogarty

Project Name: Grid Zero
Team: Solo
Path: Gameplay Path
Engine : Unreal Engine 5 
Overview

Grid Zero is a first-person shooter built in Unreal Engine 5, designed to place AI-driven (in particular clustering mechanics) combat at the center of the player experience. The central thesis of the project is that a small number of highly capable, tactically coordinated enemies create a more compelling and stressful experience than a large number of simple ones. 

I like to think of it like a one-two punch, where we flow the player into a particular pipeline and once alerted they both get swarm mechanics and different cluster mechanics on top of that, for example with our soldier clusters once one of the soldiers alerts on the player, it sends out an “Alert” to its cluster and any other nearby AIs, which causes a “collapse” onto the player while simultaneously the soldiers (which are in clusters of three), break into their respective roles of “Advance”, “Flank” and “Suppress”. 

Inspiration was drawn from the group engagement dynamics of Destiny and the individual AI experience of Escape from Tarkov, with the goal of combining the strengths of each while addressing the weaknesses of both. The result is a game built around three distinct AI archetypes, those being the Soldier, the Sniper, and the Boss, each of which exerts different kinds of pressure on the player, and which together create compound tactical problems that demand deliberate, focused play.

The intended player experience is one of consistent tension. Moving through the level carelessly should be punished quickly and harshly, while a patient and perceptive player is rewarded. The map funnels the player toward the central objective, an extraction orb located at the far end of the level with the placement of enemy clusters, snipers, and bosses is designed to punish poor movement and engagement decisions. The combination of AI that is designed to create pressure from multiple angles simultaneously and the player’s low health pool (the player can be killed by two sniper rounds, or two boss melee attacks), requires the player to make tactical decisions moving through the map. 



AI Architecture and Design 

Each of the three enemy archetypes, the Soldier, the Sniper, and the Boss was designed to stress the player in a distinct way, and the AI systems were built specifically to deliver on those design intentions. All three share a common perception and state management foundation, which meant that the work of tuning how enemies see and respond to the player only needed to happen once, and improvements cascaded across all types. Each archetype then layers its own movement and combat behavior on top of that shared base.

The most impactful design decision in the project was the alert propagation and cluster coordination system that governs soldier behavior. The core idea is simple: the moment one soldier spots the player, the entire cluster of three immediately reorganizes around a coordinated response. One soldier closes directly on the player's position, one attempts to approach from the flank, and one holds at distance and fires to suppress movement. The result is that the player cannot respond to any single threat without creating exposure to another. Hiding from the advancing soldier means taking ranged fire from the suppressor, breaking LOS with the suppressor creates an opening for the flanker. The only reliable paths through a soldier encounter is to eliminate one enemy quickly enough so that the pressure becomes manageable, to constantly run away while shooting to keep the soldiers in their “chasing/convergence” behavior, or to eliminate the soldier/soldiers from a distance outside of their detection range. This is where the Sniper and Boss roles along with the alert system come into play in creating a full high pressure experience for the player, the Sniper will create long range pressure on the player, not allow them to stand out in the open to pick off soldiers out of range, the Boss will add to the swarm and begin to chase down the player and block off paths, and the alert system will propagate, for as an alert AI comes into close proximity of another AI it will provide that AI with the player location and “activate” that AI, even if that AI never saw the player for itself. 

Both the boss and sniper have space holding technics, the sniper “flee” mechanic, which causes the sniper to fall back if it gets too close to the player, allows the sniper to hold space (some snipers were placed on the ground and some in designed elevated locations, those elevated locations provide no exit for the sniper, so it will never flee and always will hold that space), I have also made the sniper health pool extremely high, so that the player cannot execute (feasably) them from outside their range. The boss, through its design of extremely low speed and particular location starting points, and most importantly its detuned alert system (too prevent the boss from getting pulled into far away fights, I significantly detuned its alert range so that it holds the spaces it was spawned in but still reacts if the player is being engaged in close but out of view range). These three components, the sniper holding long sight lines, the boss blocking and funneling player lanes and the soldiers working as a mobile cluster to dynamically fill in the map gaps as the player works through the game allows for a consistently high pressure experience. 	
Improvements to be made 

	One obvious improvement that I would like to make in the future of this game would be to “polish” and refine the user experience, in particular map, AI, and first person visuals. This course was my first introduction to game design, and outside of the five assignments in this course I've never worked with a game engine before, so I felt that throughout the design and implementation of my game that I would focus on algorithms and AI behavior as that is where I felt more comfortable. I did play around with attempting to add in a gun to the first person perspective, which did not go well and which was quickly abandoned in order to focus on refining the AI mechanics. However, now looking at my finished project it feels more like a wireframe of a game then a fleshed out implementation, I really like my map design in terms of flow, AI spawn points/positioning and the flow of those AI through the map, but it would be really nice to have non-generic AI character models that have legs that move as they walk, and scenery beyond yellow and white blocks. 

	Another improvement I would like to focus on would be to add sound mechanics to the game, in particular damage, walking, alerting and firearm sounds. I feel that each of these would help to make the user experience “come alive”, with damage sounds you increase the perceived anxiety and stress levels of the player, walking and alerting sounds would provide the user with a more complete experience, where suddenly a cluster or boss isn't tracking behind the user without any indication made to the user of their presence, and finally firearm sounds would help the user not feel disconnected from their experience of engaging with and eliminating enemies, where right now it might just feel very “point and clicky” without any feedback being passed back to the user. 

	Along the same lines of sound improvements, I also wanted to experiment with foggy/non-loading zones that would surround the user and make them feel like they are always just a step or corner away from running into a cluster or boss, and have sniper fire coming out from the shadows to encourage proper cover placement and line picking. However, it was not possible to follow down this pathway as without sound to provide more user sensory input, removing their visual input would render the game unpleasant and unenjoyable, as they would lose through no fault of their own. 

Results and Reflection 

	Now that I have played a lot of my game, and I mean a lot as I had to more or less tune each AI as it was created, and then had to tune them again once I built and populated the map in order to provide a consistent user experience, I think I am really happy with how it turned out and in particular the feeling of pressure on the user when you mess up or make a mistake engaging with the AI. I realized I had got the game in a state that I found enjoyable when I was doing a speedrun to test the time recording and scoreboard and made a mistake which resulted in me accidently triggered two soldier clusters, 2 snipers and a boss at once, when I looked behind me and saw the train of AIs chasing it threw me right back to playing COD zombies as a kid and “running a train” to stay alive. I realized that I had created the same player loop where you have to “run a train” in my game when you make mistakes just like in zombies so that you can eliminate AIs when their stuck in an engagement loop and it was really cool to recreate a similar experience that I’ve gotten in AAA games in my own.

Overall, although there are areas of improvement that I would like to focus on and places where I'm going to flesh out this game more, I am extremely happy with the way that I created the clustering and alert systems, and they way they are able to work together to create a consistently high pressure environment to create a pleasurable user experience for those who desire it. The clustering and alert systems, the role assignment mechanic, the sniper's spatial pressure, and the boss's territorial design all came together to create exactly the experience I set out to build, where a single mistake immediately creates that feeling of being swarmed and attacked on all fronts from an unrelenting and intelligent enemy. Recreating that feeling from Call of Duty Black Ops 1 Zombies (in particular the map Kino der Toten), a game that I had sunk hundreds of hours into as a kid, that same “OH-CRAP” feeling, fills me with a lot of joy and pride. Welcome to Grid Zero!
